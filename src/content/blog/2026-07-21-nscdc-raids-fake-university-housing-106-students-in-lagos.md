**Lagos, Nigeria – July 21, 2026** – In a startling operation that highlights the persistent problem of fraudulent educational institutions in Nigeria, operatives of the Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps (NSCDC) have uncovered and shut down a suspected fake university operating in Ilado, Olorunda LCDA of Lagos State.

Gbenga Ekunola, Chief Superintendent and Area Commander of the NSCDC in Badagry, announced the raid on Monday, revealing that 106 young students were rescued from a three-bedroom apartment where the illegal institution was running its operations. 

The discovery has raised serious concerns about the exploitation of desperate youths seeking higher education in a country where access to quality tertiary institutions remains limited.

The scale of the operation — 106 students crammed into an ordinary residential building — underscores the audacity of the perpetrators. 

Fake universities and unaccredited institutions have plagued Nigeria’s education sector for years, preying on young people eager for certificates but unable to secure admission into legitimate schools due to limited spaces, high costs, or examination failures.

Authorities have not yet released full details on the operators, the courses supposedly offered, or how long the fake university had been running. 

However, the rescue of such a large number of students suggests the scam had been active for some time, potentially issuing worthless certificates that could derail the futures of those involved.

This incident is not isolated. Over the years, Nigeria has seen multiple raids on bogus schools, “degree mills,” and unaccredited study centres. 

The problem is particularly acute in urban areas like Lagos, where population pressure and economic hardship make young people easy targets for educational fraudsters promising quick qualifications.

The development also points to weaknesses in regulatory oversight by bodies like the National Universities Commission (NUC). 

Experts have repeatedly called for stronger monitoring, public awareness campaigns, and stiffer penalties for operators of illegal institutions.

For the 106 rescued students, the raid represents both a rude awakening and a potential lifeline. Many may have invested time and money into programmes that hold no academic or professional value. 

The NSCDC and relevant education authorities will need to provide counselling, verification of any certificates issued, and guidance on legitimate pathways forward.

Parents and guardians across the country are being urged to verify the accreditation status of any institution before enrolling their wards. 

The incident serves as a sobering reminder that in the pursuit of education, due diligence is essential.

As a journalist who has covered education and youth issues for decades, this story strikes a particularly painful chord. 

Nigeria’s youthful population is its greatest asset, yet too often it is exploited by those peddling false dreams. 

Every fake university uncovered represents dozens — or in this case over a hundred — dashed hopes and wasted years.

The NSCDC deserves commendation for the raid, but this must be part of a broader, sustained crackdown. 

Regulatory agencies, security forces, and the public must work together to protect young Nigerians from educational fraud. 

The goal should be a system where legitimate ambition meets genuine opportunity, not deception.
