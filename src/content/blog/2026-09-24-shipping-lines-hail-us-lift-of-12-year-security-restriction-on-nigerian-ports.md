**Lagos, Nigeria—September 24, 2026**—Leading multinational shipping companies operating in Nigeria have welcomed the United States Coast Guard’s decision to lift a 12-year Condition of Entry imposed on vessels that called at Nigerian ports before sailing to the United States, describing the move as a major boost for trade, maritime security, and the country’s standing in the global shipping industry.

The restriction, first introduced in June 2014, required ships bound for U.S. waters that had visited designated Nigerian ports within their previous five port calls to undergo additional security measures and enhanced scrutiny. The policy added costs, delays, and administrative burdens for operators and was widely seen as a drag on the competitiveness of Nigerian ports.

Minister of Marine and Blue Economy Dr. Adegboyega Oyetola announced the lifting in August after the U.S. Coast Guard completed four assessments of Nigeria’s maritime security framework and port facilities between March 2024 and April 2026. 

Officials said the reviews confirmed significant progress in compliance with the International Ship and Port Facility Security (ISPS) Code.



**Industry Reaction**

Global carriers have responded positively. Maersk’s Terminal Planning Lead for West Africa, Srijesh Subramanian, called the development “*a welcome move”* that would support both importers and exporters and encourage shipping lines to expand their plans in Nigeria. 

He noted that the decision signals improved confidence in the country’s maritime environment.

Ocean Network Express (ONE) Nigeria Director Stefan Pedersen congratulated the country and credited the Nigerian Maritime Administration and Safety Agency (NIMASA) for sustained work that has now paid off. 

Pacific International Lines (PIL) Managing Director Ugo Opiah highlighted the reputational benefit, saying qualification under U.S. standards shows Nigeria has raised its game in maritime security.

MSC and other operators similarly described the change as positive for schedule reliability, vessel turnaround times and overall trade volumes between Nigeria and the United States.



**What the Change Means**

With the Condition of Entry removed, vessels that call at Nigerian ports will no longer face the extra security requirements solely because of those calls. 

Industry observers expect lower operating costs, faster processing, and greater attractiveness of Nigerian ports to international lines. 

U.S. officials have also formally welcomed the milestone, with Assistant Secretary of State for African Affairs Frank W. Garcia Jr. congratulating Nigeria on its sustained improvements in port security and anti-terrorism compliance.

The decision marks a significant external validation of reforms pursued under the Ministry of Marine and Blue Economy and NIMASA. 

Stakeholders say it should help Nigeria compete more effectively for shipping business in the region and support broader trade and investment goals.

As the policy takes full effect, shipping executives and port operators will be watching closely for tangible gains in service frequency, cost reductions, and cargo volumes on the Nigeria–U.S. trade lane.
