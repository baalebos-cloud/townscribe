**Abuja, Nigeria—November 24, 2026**—Amnesty International Nigeria staff, supporters, and volunteers on Monday paid a solidarity visit to investigative journalist Daniel Ojukwu at Kuje Correctional Centre in Abuja, where he has been held since November 10 for reports that raised questions about the handling of public funds.

Ojukwu, a correspondent with the Foundation for Investigative Journalism (FIJ), pleaded not guilty to a 12-count charge of cyberstalking and criminal defamation. 

The case stems from a series of FIJ investigations published between November 2023 and January 2024 that examined contracts awarded by the Office of the Senior Special Assistant to the President on Sustainable Development Goals, then headed by Adejoke Orelope-Adefulire. 

One report highlighted a payment of about ₦147.1 million to a company for classroom and skills-acquisition projects at a Lagos primary school, noting that little visible work appeared to match the expenditure.

The Federal High Court in Abuja, presided over by Justice Joyce Abdulmalik, ordered Ojukwu’s remand pending the hearing of his bail application, scheduled for December 1. FIJ founder Fisayo Soyombo is named as a co-defendant.

In a post accompanying photographs from the prison visit, Amnesty International Nigeria described the charges as “*bogus”* and the trial as a “sham,” saying Ojukwu is being punished solely for exposing corruption. *“His case symbolizes the widespread criminalization of human rights defenders and journalists in Nigeria—simply exposing corruption,”* the group stated, affirming its solidarity with Ojukwu and FIJ.

The latest detention follows Ojukwu’s earlier arrest in May 2024, when he was held for about 10 days over the same reporting before public pressure secured his release. 

Press freedom groups, including the Committee to Protect Journalists, SERAP, the Nigerian Guild of Editors, and the Wole Soyinka Centre for Investigative Journalism have condemned the remand, arguing that the Cybercrimes Act and defamation laws are being used to intimidate journalists who scrutinize public spending.

Ojukwu’s supporters note that the investigations concerned matters of clear public interest—how taxpayer money was spent on development projects. 

Critics of the prosecution warn that keeping a reporter in custody while his bail application awaits hearing risks creating a climate of fear that discourages accountability journalism.

As the December 1 bail hearing approaches, the case continues to draw attention to the tension between investigative reporting and the legal tools available to public officials in Nigeria. 

Amnesty’s visit underscored a simple message: standing with a journalist who asked hard questions about public funds.
