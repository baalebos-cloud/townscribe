**Ibadan, Oyo State| July 22, 2026-** In a significant judicial ruling that underscores the delicate balance between anti-corruption efforts and constitutional protections, the Federal High Court in Ibadan, Oyo State, has restrained the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC) from proceeding with its planned investigation into the Oyo State Government's finances.

Delivering judgment in a suit filed by the Oyo State Government, Justice Nkeonye Maha acknowledged the EFCC’s statutory powers to investigate financial crimes but stressed that such authority must be exercised strictly within the bounds of the Constitution and the rule of law. 

The court described the EFCC’s demands as speculative and amounting to a "fishing expedition," warning that they risked trampling on the plaintiffs' rights to fair hearing.

The dispute stemmed from a letter dated June 2, 2025, sent by the EFCC to the Oyo State Accountant-General. It demanded extensive documentation, including copies of all state contracts and details of transactions and payments to contractors from 2021 onward. 

Oyo State officials, led by Attorney General Barrister Abiodun Aikomo, argued that the broad request was oppressive, unduly burdensome, and unreasonable given the sheer volume of records involved.

EFCC counsel defended the action by citing Section 38 of the EFCC Act (2004), which empowers the commission to demand information from any authority without hindrance. 

However, Justice Maha ruled in favour of Oyo State, setting aside the EFCC’s letter and declaring that any future requests must be reasonable, specific, and tied to genuine allegations of offences under the Act — not roving or speculative inquiries.

This ruling comes amid ongoing tensions between federal anti-graft agencies and state governments, particularly in opposition-led states like Oyo under Governor Seyi Makinde. 

It highlights broader debates about the limits of EFCC powers, the autonomy of states, and the need for targeted, evidence-based investigations rather than wide-net approaches.

Supporters of the decision view it as a necessary check against potential overreach, protecting governance from undue interference while still affirming the EFCC's role in combating corruption. 

Critics, however, may see it as a setback for transparency efforts, especially given past scrutiny over state finances, relief funds, and contract awards in various administrations.

Oyo State, which has positioned itself as a model of prudent management under Makinde, celebrated the outcome as a victory for due process. 

The judgment reinforces that while anti-corruption bodies have teeth, their actions cannot bypass constitutional safeguards.

The EFCC has faced similar legal pushbacks in recent years, with courts occasionally faulting its methods in high-profile cases. 

This Ibadan ruling adds to precedents emphasising specificity in probes and could influence future interactions between the commission and sub-national governments.

As Nigeria grapples with economic challenges, insecurity, and public demands for accountability, such judicial interventions serve as reminders of the importance of checks and balances in a federal democracy. 

The EFCC may yet appeal, but for now, Oyo State breathes easier — and the conversation on collaborative, rule-bound governance continues.
