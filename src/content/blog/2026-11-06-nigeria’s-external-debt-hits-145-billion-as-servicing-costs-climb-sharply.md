**Abuja, Nigeria—November 6, 2026—**Nigeria's external debt stock climbed to $145.015 billion in 2025, marking a 6.6 percent increase from the previous year, according to the World Bank’s International Debt Report 2026. The figure represents a substantial jump of $61.8 billion from the $83.213 billion recorded in 2015, a 74.3 percent rise over the decade.

The report, which tracks external borrowing and repayment trends across low- and middle-income countries, provides a detailed breakdown of Nigeria’s obligations. 

Public and publicly guaranteed debt accounted for $62.4 billion of the total. Private nonguaranteed debt stood at $54.476 billion, while short-term external debt reached $28.139 billion.

Multilateral creditors held the largest share of Nigeria’s public and publicly guaranteed external debt at $31.365 billion. Private creditors followed with $24.352 billion, and bilateral creditors accounted for $6.683 billion. 

Within the multilateral category, the World Bank’s International Development Association (IDA) was the biggest single source at $18.533 billion. IMF Special Drawing Rights allocations contributed another $5.516 billion.

China remained Nigeria’s largest bilateral creditor with $5.672 billion, followed by France at $774 million and other bilateral lenders with $237 million.



**Mixed Signals on Debt Sustainability**

Despite the overall increase in the debt stock, Nigeria recorded improvements in two key sustainability indicators. 

External debt relative to exports declined by 4.3 per cent to 212 per cent. The debt-to-Gross National Income (GNI) ratio also fell by 6.6 per cent to 51 per cent.

However, the cost of servicing the debt moved in the opposite direction. External debt service relative to exports rose by 9.6 per cent to 19 per cent. Debt service as a proportion of GNI increased by 6.9 per cent to five per cent. 

These figures indicate that while the size of the debt relative to the economy and export earnings improved slightly, the actual burden of making payments grew heavier.

The World Bank noted that debt-service payments among all low- and middle-income countries reached $1.4 trillion in 2025, the fifth consecutive year of increases. 

Payments rose by 1.9 per cent, driven largely by obligations to private creditors, particularly international bondholders. 

Between 2022 and 2025, these countries paid approximately $436.1 billion more in debt servicing than they received in new financing. 

The trend reversed slightly in 2025, when long-term debt flows recorded a net inflow of $45.4 billion after a net outflow of $118.4 billion the previous year.



**Higher Cost of Private Borrowing**

The report highlighted a sharp difference in borrowing costs. New commitments from official creditors in 2025 carried an average interest rate of 1.9 per cent, with a maturity of 25 years and a five-year grace period. 

In contrast, private creditors charged an average interest rate of 9.6 per cent, with only a 12-year maturity and an eight-year grace period.

Additional details from related coverage noted that Nigeria entered into a $5 billion Total Return Swap arrangement in 2026, pledging about $6.7 billion in domestic government securities as collateral. 

While the facility provides quick foreign currency liquidity, the World Bank cautioned that such structures carry margin call, refinancing, and transparency risks.

Economists and fiscal analysts say the rising servicing burden continues to limit the government’s fiscal space for critical investments in infrastructure, health, education, and social programs. 

The data underscores the challenge of balancing the need for external financing with the long-term sustainability of repayment obligations in an environment of elevated global interest rates and tighter credit conditions for emerging markets.
