**Lagos / Abuja – August 8, 2026—American** missionary and humanitarian Alex Barbir has issued a stark appeal to Americans and world leaders, describing horrific scenes of violence he says he witnessed in Nigeria, including babies burned alive, entire villages destroyed, and families wiped out in single nighttime attacks.

In a widely circulating video, Barbir, founder of the faith-based organization Building Zion, claimed that more Christians are being killed in Nigeria than in every other country combined. 

He expressed frustration that too few voices are speaking up for the victims.

*“Statements like this force a difficult conversation,”* noted Nigerian commentator Somto Okonkwo, who shared the video. “*Large-scale violence against Christian communities has been a recurring tragedy in parts of Nigeria for years.”*



**A Pattern of Brutality in the Middle Belt**

Barbir has spent significant time in conflict-affected areas of Plateau, Benue, Kaduna and Taraba states. He has documented attacks he attributes largely to armed Fulani militants, describing mass graves, burned homes and the recovery of skeletal remains of children.

Independent data lends weight to the scale of the crisis. Open Doors’ World Watch List has repeatedly shown Nigeria accounting for the overwhelming majority of faith-related Christian killings worldwide — in one recent reporting period, 3,490 of the 4,849 Christians killed globally for their faith died in Nigeria.

Local reports and human rights groups have recorded repeated massacres in farming communities, with victims often including women, children, and entire households. 

One widely reported 2025 attack in Yelwata, Benue State, left scores dead, with survivors describing people locked in buildings and set ablaze.



**Government Response and Contested Narratives**

Barbir has been sharply critical of successive Nigerian governments, accusing authorities of failing to protect vulnerable citizens and of downplaying the religious dimension of the violence. 

Nigerian officials have rejected characterizations of the crisis as a targeted *“Christian genocide,*” framing much of the bloodshed as complex farmer-herder conflicts, banditry, and general insecurity that also claims Muslim lives.

In April 2026, reports emerged that Barbir’s visa was revoked and he was ordered to leave Nigeria after authorities accused him of making statements capable of inflaming tensions. 

He has disputed aspects of the official account.



**Calls for Equal Urgency**

Commentators responding to Barbir’s latest remarks stressed that every Nigerian life lost to violence deserves equal attention and protection, regardless of religion or ethnicity. 

The underlying reality of insecurity, targeted attacks on rural communities, and the state’s struggle to guarantee safety remains a national emergency.

As images and testimonies from the Middle Belt continue to surface, Barbir’s appeal underscores a painful truth: for many communities, the violence is not abstract statistics but lived trauma—burned homes, missing children, and nights of terror that demand sustained national and international attention.
