In a passionate and unfiltered video message that is rapidly gaining traction across Nigerian social media, prominent human rights lawyer and activist Dele Farotimi has issued a stark warning to citizens: political disengagement is not an option. 

Farotimi cautioned that those who consider themselves "too big" for politics risk ceding power to unprepared or undesirable figures, using Lagos transport union leader MC Oluomo as a vivid example.

*"If you think you’re too big for politics, MC Oluomo will be your next governor. Mushroom pikin will govern your state if you exclude yourself from its affairs,*" Farotimi declared in the clip, employing blunt, relatable language to drive home his point. 

The term "mushroom pikin" — slang for someone who emerges suddenly without deep roots or preparation — underscores his critique of opportunistic leadership rising in the vacuum left by voter apathy.

Farotimi, known for his sharp critiques of Nigeria's political system and advocacy for accountability, emphasized that democracy demands active participation from all segments of society, especially the educated and professional classes. 

He argued that staying aloof from the process—limiting involvement to post-election criticism — allows less accountable individuals to shape the nation's future.

His remarks come at a time of widespread frustration with governance in Nigeria. Persistent challenges including economic hardship, insecurity, and questions over electoral integrity have fueled disillusionment. 

Farotimi has long described Nigeria's elections as more ritual than genuine choice, pointing to low voter turnout in past cycles (such as under 20 million actual votes from nearly 90 million registered in 2023) as enabling manipulation, vote-buying, and suppression.

*"Political participation is a responsibility for citizens who desire better governance,"* he stressed. 

By urging professionals to engage rather than spectate, Farotimi aims to spark a broader awakening ahead of future polls, including the critical 2027 elections.

A vocal critic of systemic failures, Farotimi has consistently called for deeper reforms. 

He has described activism alone as insufficient, advocating instead for a "peaceful revolution" rooted in collective citizen action, electoral overhaul (including independent candidacy), and grassroots awareness. 

His messages often blend legal insight with cultural resonance, making complex issues accessible — as seen in his repeated pleas for Nigerians to *"make peace with your PVC"* (Permanent Voter Card).

MC Oluomo, referenced in the warning, is a polarizing figure as chairman of the National Union of Road Transport Workers (NURTW) in Lagos. 

Critics have accused him of wielding significant influence in local politics, sometimes through controversial means, while supporters view him as a grassroots mobilizer. 

Farotimi's choice of reference highlights concerns about *"godfatherism"* and the elevation of figures from non-traditional political backgrounds.

Farotimi's intervention taps into ongoing national conversations about leadership quality, voter turnout, and the health of Nigeria's democracy. 

With economic pressures mounting and public trust in institutions strained, his call resonates with many who feel disconnected from a system perceived as serving elite interests.

Observers note that such warnings are timely. Low engagement historically benefits entrenched powers, while higher participation could pressure for better accountability. 

Farotimi's message is not partisan but a broader plea for ownership: Nigerians must shape their destiny rather than abdicate it.

As reactions pour in online—from agreement to debate over his examples—one thing is clear: Farotimi continues to provoke thought and action in a polity hungry for change. 

Whether his words translate into higher turnout and renewed civic energy remains to be seen, but they serve as a timely reminder that in democracy, silence has consequences.
