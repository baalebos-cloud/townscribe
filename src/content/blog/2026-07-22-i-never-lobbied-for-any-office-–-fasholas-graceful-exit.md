In a rare, introspective interview that has resonated deeply across Nigeria’s political landscape, former Lagos State Governor and ex-Minister of Works and Housing, Babatunde Raji Fashola, SAN, has opened up about his decision to step away from public office without lobbying for more. 

The 63-year-old legal luminary, widely regarded as one of Nigeria’s most effective administrators, described his journey as one of profound privilege and luck, free from the aggressive politicking that often defines careers in the arena.

Speaking candidly on TVC News Nigeria’s ***Beyond The Headlines***, Fashola addressed questions about political retirement and ambition head-on. 

*“I have been very lucky. I haven’t lobbied for any office in which I have served,”* he stated, emphasizing that each role — from Chief of Staff to Governor of Lagos and federal minister — came through merit and opportunity rather than self-promotion.

Born on June 28, 1963, Fashola rose through the ranks as a respected lawyer before entering public service. As Chief of Staff to then-Governor Bola Tinubu (2003-2007), he gained invaluable experience. 

Elected Governor of Lagos in 2007 and re-elected in 2011, he spearheaded sweeping reforms that modernized Africa’s largest city. 

Key achievements included infrastructure overhauls like the Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system, Lekki-Epe Expressway expansions, the Eko Atlantic project, security initiatives via the Lagos State Security Trust Fund, and effective Ebola containment in 2014.

His tax reforms dramatically boosted internally generated revenue, laying foundations for Lagos’ economic resilience. 

Internationally acclaimed, Fashola earned plaudits from outlets like TIME and The Economist for turning Lagos into a model of urban governance.

In 2015, President Muhammadu Buhari appointed him Minister of Power, Works and Housing (later split). 

Over eight years, he delivered critical national projects: advancing the Second Niger Bridge, rehabilitating thousands of kilometers of roads, and pushing power sector improvements despite challenges. 

President Tinubu recently hailed him on his 63rd birthday for his “remarkable contributions to public service, governance, and national development.”

Fashola tied his exit partly to turning 60 in 2023 — a personal milestone aligning with 35 years since leaving school — and the end of his administration’s term. “*The stress was beginning to tell,”* he admitted, reflecting on two decades of intense service. 

Now back in legal practice, he finds fulfillment in a quieter life, looking back with gratitude rather than regret.

He firmly dismissed presidential ambitions: “No,” he responded emphatically when asked, calling his past roles an “unimaginable privilege” that he hopes inspires younger generations. 

Fashola stressed the importance of strong opposition, acceptance of electoral outcomes despite imperfections, and allowing fresh talent to emerge.

His measured tone stands in contrast to the often frantic lobbying in Nigerian politics, earning praise from supporters who see him as a model of integrity and strategic withdrawal.

Fashola’s reflections come amid ongoing discussions about leadership renewal, youth inclusion, and sustainable governance ahead of future cycles. 

At a time when many cling to power, his graceful exit offers a powerful counter-narrative: public service as a chapter, not a lifetime entitlement.

As Nigeria grapples with infrastructure deficits, economic pressures, and democratic strengthening, voices like Fashola's—rooted in proven delivery rather than rhetoric—remain influential. 

Whether through private counsel, legal expertise, or occasional public commentary, his legacy of competence endures.

In an era of short attention spans and polarized discourse, Fashola’s story reminds us that true impact often lies in knowing when to step forward—and when to step back with dignity.
