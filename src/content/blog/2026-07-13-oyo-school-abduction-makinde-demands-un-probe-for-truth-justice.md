**Ibadan, Oyo State – July 13, 2026** — In a bold and emotionally charged address, Oyo State Governor Seyi Makinde has urged the United Nations and international human rights organizations to launch an independent investigation into the harrowing May 15 abduction of dozens of schoolchildren and teachers in Oriire Local Government Area. 

Describing the incident as one of "sufficiently grave and unusual" circumstances, Makinde emphasized that Nigerians deserve full transparency, not political spin.

*"This is about justice, not politics,"* the governor declared, underscoring a commitment to accountability that transcends partisan lines. 

His call comes just days after security forces rescued the surviving victims following a traumatic 56-day captivity in the forests of Old Oyo National Park.

On May 15, 2026, armed gunmen—widely reported as terrorists linked to groups like Ansaru—launched simultaneous assaults on three schools in the Ahoro-Esinle and Yawota communities: Community High School, L.A. Primary School, and Yawota Baptist Nursery and Primary School.

Initial reports indicated between 39 and 46 pupils and seven teachers were taken, including school principal Mrs. Rachael Folawe Alamu. 

The abduction shocked the nation, evoking painful memories of past school kidnappings in Nigeria's northeast while highlighting the spread of such violence to the southwest.

During captivity, the ordeal turned deadly. At least two teachers, including Mr. Michael Oyedokun, were killed—one reportedly on the second day. 

Survivors endured immense hardship in the forest hideout, with accounts of brutality emerging from rescued individuals.

Families waited in anguish for nearly two months. Governor Makinde met with them repeatedly, coordinating with federal security agencies while navigating the delicate balance between negotiation possibilities and military action. 

The rescue, described as an intelligence-led operation by the Nigerian military and other agencies, resulted in the safe recovery of the remaining victims. Eight kidnappers were reportedly arrested.

Upon receiving the rescued pupils and teachers at his office in Ibadan, Makinde praised the bravery of security operatives, including the Nigerian Army, for their sacrifices. 

He outlined immediate support: medical evaluations, psychological care at facilities like the Nigerian Army Medical Services (2 Division), family reunifications, and long-term rehabilitation.

Yet, the governor refused to treat the matter as closed. Highlighting the federal government's primary responsibility for national security, Makinde stressed that the scale, coordination, and brutality of the attack demanded external oversight. 

He called for the UN and global bodies to document abuses, uncover operational lapses, and ensure justice.

*"This incident raises serious questions that must be answered,"* sources close to the governor's office noted, reflecting broader concerns about intelligence failures, border porosity, and the evolving threat of banditry and terrorism in what was once considered a relatively stable region.

Nigeria has faced a surge in school abductions, from the infamous Chibok girls in 2014 to more recent incidents. 

The Oyo attack coincided with other security challenges, amplifying public frustration with systemic issues like poverty, unemployment, and weak rural policing.

Analysts point to nuances: while the rescue is a victory, the deaths in captivity, prolonged trauma for young victims, and questions about how such a coordinated operation evaded detection expose vulnerabilities. 

Politically, Makinde's push for a UN probe could test federal-state relations, especially as 2027 elections loom and opposition voices scrutinize security handling.

Implications ripple outward. For education in rural areas, fear may deter enrollment. For families, the psychological scars will linger, requiring sustained community and governmental support. 

Edge cases include potential copycat attacks or the risk of victims' testimonies being politicized.

Human rights advocates welcome the transparency call, viewing it as a rare high-level endorsement of international accountability in domestic security matters. 

Critics, however, may see it as undermining national sovereignty—though Makinde framed it firmly as a quest for truth.

As victims begin recovery, Oyo State has vowed tighter security around vulnerable sites like Old Oyo Park. Makinde's administration continues collaborating with federal forces while urging citizens to remain vigilant.

This episode underscores Nigeria's complex security landscape: triumphs in rescue operations tempered by the need for deeper systemic reforms. 

Full transparency, as Makinde insists, could not only deliver justice for Oyo's families but set a precedent for addressing similar tragedies nationwide.

The international community now watches closely. Will the UN respond? How will Abuja engage? For now, the focus remains on the resilient survivors and a governor determined to turn pain into purposeful reform.
