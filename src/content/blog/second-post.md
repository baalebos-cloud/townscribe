As Ekiti State inches closer to the 2026 governorship election, an unsettling reality is beginning to unfold — one that speaks less of governance and more of intimidation, suppression, and political desperation. What should have been a season of democratic engagement is fast becoming a theatre of fear, where dissent is treated like a crime and opposition voices are hunted with alarming intensity.

At the center of the latest controversy is Hon. Ayodele Babatola, the African Democratic Congress (ADC) House of Assembly candidate for Ado-Ekiti Constituency I and son of the party’s Deputy Governorship candidate. His arrest and swift remand over rape allegations have ignited outrage across political circles, not merely because of the severity of the accusation, but because of the suspicious speed and timing surrounding the process.

In a justice system where countless serious cases crawl endlessly through procedural bottlenecks, the rapid handling of Babatola’s case has raised difficult questions. To many observers, it appears less like justice in motion and more like power in panic mode.

No responsible society jokes with allegations of rape. Such accusations deserve serious, transparent, and uncompromising investigation. But justice loses its credibility when it appears selective — when political opponents are pursued with lightning speed while broader societal failures remain ignored. The principle of innocence until proven guilty cannot suddenly become negotiable simply because politics is involved.

What is unfolding in Ekiti increasingly resembles a calculated pattern rather than isolated incidents.

Opposition figures allege harassment. Critics speak of intimidation. Protesters complain of arrests. Supporters of rival political interests describe raids, threats, and phantom charges. Across the state, there is a growing perception that security agencies are no longer acting as neutral protectors of law and order, but as instruments deployed to shield political interests and weaken dissent.

One of the most controversial symbols of this alleged abuse is the notorious “Form K” remand process — a mechanism critics say has become a shortcut for detention without sufficient evidence. What should exist as a legal safeguard is now widely viewed as a political weapon, particularly against those perceived as threats to the ruling establishment.

The arrest of Akogun Abayomi, popularly known as “Lustay,” deepened these suspicions. Once a government insider, his troubles reportedly began after openly aligning with APC governorship aspirant Kayode Ojo. Suddenly, he found himself facing grave criminal allegations ranging from armed robbery to attempted murder — accusations his supporters insist are politically motivated.

Then came the controversy surrounding the YSJ agricultural initiative. Young participants who protested alleged failures and mismanagement within the scheme reportedly found themselves facing arrests instead of answers. Citizens who demanded accountability were treated not as stakeholders, but as enemies.

Taken together, these events paint a troubling picture of a government increasingly intolerant of criticism and deeply anxious about political competition.

A truly confident administration does not fear opposition. It does not silence critics, weaponize institutions, or criminalize dissent. Strong leadership earns loyalty through performance, transparency, and public trust — not intimidation.

And this is perhaps the most troubling question confronting Governor Biodun Oyebanji’s administration today: if the government’s record truly speaks for itself, why does criticism provoke such aggressive reactions? Why does opposition appear threatening enough to justify arrests, detentions, and political hostility?

Democracy was never designed to be comfortable for those in power. It demands tolerance, accountability, and the acceptance that no government is beyond scrutiny. Once state institutions begin to serve political interests instead of public justice, democracy itself begins to erode.

Power, after all, is temporary. History remembers not only those who governed, but how they governed — whether they protected freedoms or crushed them, whether they inspired confidence or ruled through fear.

The people of Ekiti deserve a political atmosphere where disagreement is not treated as rebellion, where young voices can protest without intimidation, and where elections are contested with ideas instead of coercion.

If the allegations against any accused individual are genuine, then due process must take its full course. But if the machinery of justice is being manipulated for political advantage, then that should alarm every citizen regardless of party affiliation. Today’s target may be an opposition figure; tomorrow, it could be anyone who dares to speak too loudly.

As the 2026 elections approach, the true test of leadership in Ekiti will not simply be who wins power, but whether democracy itself survives the struggle for it.
