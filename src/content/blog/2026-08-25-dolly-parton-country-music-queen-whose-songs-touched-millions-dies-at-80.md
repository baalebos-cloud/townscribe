**NASHVILLE** — Dolly Parton, the irrepressible country music superstar whose songs of heartbreak, resilience, and hard-won joy became the soundtrack for generations, died Tuesday in Nashville. She was 80.

Her nephew Bryan Seaver, who long served as her head of security, announced the news in a video posted to her social media accounts. 

“*This video announcement is something Dolly asked of me years ago*,” Seaver said. No cause of death was given. A private family service is planned.

Parton had faced a series of health challenges in recent years. She postponed and later canceled a planned Las Vegas residency, citing immune and digestive system problems, recurring kidney stones, dizziness, and dehydration. 

In a final interview days before her death, she spoke of neglecting her own health while caring for her husband, Carl Dean, who died in March 2025 after nearly 60 years of marriage.

Born Dolly Rebecca Parton on Jan. 19, 1946, in a one-room cabin in Locust Ridge, Tennessee, she was the fourth of 12 children in a family that knew real poverty. 

Her father farmed and worked construction; her mother filled the home with music and storytelling. Those early years inspired some of her most enduring songs, including “*Coat of Many Colors”* and “*My Tennessee Mountain Home.”*

Parton began performing as a child and moved to Nashville the day after high school graduation in 1964. 

She soon joined Porter Wagoner’s television show, launching a fruitful but eventually confining partnership. In 1974 she left to pursue a solo career, writing *“I Will Always Love You*” as a farewell. 

The song later became a global phenomenon when Whitney Houston recorded it for the 1992 film \*The Bodyguard\*.

Hits such as “*Jolene,” “9 to 5,” “Here You Come Again,"* and the Kenny Rogers duet *“Islands in the Stream”* established her as one of the most successful crossover artists in country music history. 

She sold more than 100 million records, won 11 Grammy Awards (including a Lifetime Achievement honor), and earned induction into the Country Music Hall of Fame and the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame.

Beyond music, Parton built an empire. In 1986 she opened Dollywood, the theme park in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee, that became a major economic engine for her home region. 

She starred in films including **9 to 5, Steel Magnolias, a**nd **The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas**. Yet many who knew her work best point to her quiet philanthropy as her greatest legacy.

Through the Dollywood Foundation’s Imagination Library, launched in 1995, she has distributed more than 300 million free books to preschool children around the world. 

She donated $1 million to Vanderbilt University research that aided the development of the Moderna COVID-19 vaccine and consistently supported literacy, disaster relief, and her East Tennessee community.

Parton married Carl Dean, a private asphalt contractor she met at a Nashville laundromat, in 1966. He largely stayed out of the spotlight; she often said he was the steady center of her extraordinary life. The couple had no children.

In an era of cultural division, Parton remained a rare figure who seemed to unite people. Her blend of glamour and grit, humor and sincerity, made her feel both larger than life and deeply familiar. 

As one observer put it years ago, she was something almost everyone could agree on.

She is survived by her extended family. In the video announcing her death, Seaver recalled that Parton once said she wanted to rest *“in a beautiful bed of plush cotton”* after a lifetime spent in heavy rhinestones.

The music—and the books, the jobs, the hope she left behind—will keep shining long after the sequins fade.
