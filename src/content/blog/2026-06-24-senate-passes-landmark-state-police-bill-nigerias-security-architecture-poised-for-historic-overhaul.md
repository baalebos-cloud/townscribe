**Abuja, Nigeria – June 24, 2026** – In a decisive move that could reshape the nation's long-troubled security landscape, the Nigerian Senate on Wednesday passed a constitutional amendment bill enabling the establishment of state police forces across the federation. 

The vote marks a significant victory for proponents of decentralized policing and fulfills a key push by President Bola Tinubu's administration to confront pervasive threats like banditry, kidnapping, and insurgency more effectively at the local level.

The bill, formally part of the Constitution (Sixth Alteration) Bill, 2026, amends relevant provisions of the 1999 Constitution (as amended), particularly shifting policing powers from the Exclusive Legislative List to the Concurrent List. 

This change paves the way for states to create and operate their own police services alongside the federal Nigeria Police Force, introducing a dual policing structure tailored to regional realities.

The passage comes after years of intense national debate. Advocates have long argued that the centralized federal police system—often stretched thin and disconnected from local contexts—has struggled to respond swiftly to region-specific security challenges. 

Governors, in particular, have frequently complained about limited control over law enforcement in their states, leading to delayed responses and accountability gaps.

The House of Representatives had already overwhelmingly approved a similar amendment earlier in June (with 289 votes in favor and minimal opposition), demonstrating broad legislative consensus. 

President Tinubu transmitted the executive-backed bill to the Senate on June 15, prompting an emergency plenary to accelerate its consideration. Senate President Godswill Akpabio emphasized the urgency, noting that state assemblies had pledged rapid review.

During proceedings, the Senate adopted the bill's clauses in the Committee of the Whole, achieving the required two-thirds majority for constitutional alterations. 

Details include provisions for State Police Service Commissions, gubernatorial oversight with safeguards, national minimum standards, and conditions for federal intervention in cases of breakdown of law and order.

For many Nigerians weary of insecurity, this development sparks hope. "State police may help Nigeria fight insecurity closer to the ground," one observer noted, highlighting potential improvements in intelligence gathering, rapid response, and community trust. 

In states plagued by banditry in the Northwest, farmer-herder clashes in the Middle Belt, or separatist agitations in the Southeast, localized forces could prove transformative.

Yet, the reform is not without risks. Critics warn of potential politicization, with fears that powerful governors could weaponize state police against political opponents, ethnic minorities, or dissenters. 

*"The same governor fighting bandits today could be tempted to fight opponents tomorro*w," one commentator cautioned. 

There are also concerns about uneven funding, training standards, inter-state coordination, and the risk of fragmentation in an already diverse federation.

Senate leaders have stressed built-in safeguards, including federal oversight mechanisms and the reality that today's governors will eventually become tomorrow's citizens subject to the same system. 

Implementation details—such as recruitment, command structures, and funding models—will be critical.

The bill now proceeds to Nigeria's 36 state Houses of Assembly, where it requires approval by at least two-thirds (24 states) before President Tinubu can assent it into law. 

Once enacted, states could begin establishing their forces, potentially including entities reminiscent of "Lagos State Police Department" or similar localized outfits.

This reform arrives amid broader conversations about true federalism, resource control, and governance restructuring. 

Supporters see it as a pragmatic step toward addressing Nigeria's complex security realities in the 21st century; skeptics urge vigilance to ensure it serves the public rather than entrenching elite power.

As the dust settles on this pivotal Senate session, one thing is clear: Nigeria stands at a crossroads in its approach to law and order. 

Whether state police become a bulwark against chaos or a new vector for abuse will depend on the wisdom, transparency, and accountability applied in the months and years ahead. For a nation yearning for peace and stability, the stakes could hardly be higher.
