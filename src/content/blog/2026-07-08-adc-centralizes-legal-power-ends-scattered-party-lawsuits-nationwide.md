**ABUJA, Nigeria** — In a decisive step toward enhancing internal discipline and legal coherence, the African Democratic Congress (ADC) has issued a firm directive centralizing all party-related litigation at the national level. This development signals the party’s determination to present a unified front as it positions itself for the 2027 general elections.

In a public notice signed by National Publicity Secretary Hon. Bolaji Abdullahi on July 8, 2026, the ADC instructed all state chapters, executive members, and officials to immediately forward any court processes served on the party to the National Legal Adviser in Abuja. 

The statement emphasizes that only the National Legal Adviser is constitutionally authorized to engage lawyers, instruct counsel, or represent the party in legal proceedings.

The directive comes against the backdrop of ongoing internal leadership disputes and external legal challenges facing the ADC. 

By prohibiting state chapters from independently receiving court papers or engaging legal practitioners without headquarters approval, the party aims to prevent fragmented responses that could undermine its position in court. Violations may attract disciplinary action under the party’s constitution.

The notice explicitly states that service on any state chapter or officer will not be considered valid service on the national party, urging immediate electronic transmission of all processes to Abuja. 

This move is expected to reduce confusion, ensure professional handling of cases, and project a more organized national structure.

The ADC, which has been gaining attention as a potential platform for prominent opposition figures including Atiku Abubakar, appears focused on consolidating control as the political landscape heats up. 

Centralizing legal authority could help the party navigate complex litigations more effectively while presenting a cohesive image to voters and the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC).

Political observers view this as a pragmatic governance reform within the party, addressing past issues where decentralized actions led to contradictions or vulnerabilities. 

For a relatively young but ambitious party, such measures demonstrate seriousness about institutional strength and readiness for national competition. 

As Nigeria’s democratic process evolves, parties that prioritize internal order and legal prudence are better positioned to earn public confidence. 

The ADC’s latest directive may serve as a model for opposition cohesion in the run-up to 2027.

This proactive step reflects a maturing political organization committed to discipline — a positive signal in a landscape often marked by fragmentation. 

For supporters and watchers alike, it underscores the ADC’s resolve to build a credible alternative focused on unity and strategic preparedness.
