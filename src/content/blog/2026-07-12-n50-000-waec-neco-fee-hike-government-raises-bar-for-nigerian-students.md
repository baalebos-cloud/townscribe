**ABUJA, Nigeria** — In a move that could reshape the educational landscape for millions of Nigerian families, the Federal Government has officially approved a uniform registration fee of N50,000 for candidates sitting the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE) conducted by both the West African Examinations Council (WAEC) and the National Examinations Council (NECO), effective from 2027.

The decision, conveyed through a memo dated June 18, 2026, and signed by Adeniji Ibrahim, Director of Senior Secondary Education at the Federal Ministry of Education, follows a request from WAEC for a fee review and a March 31, 2026, meeting where the Minister of Education directed both bodies to harmonize their charges.

Currently, WAEC fees stand at approximately N27,000 while NECO is around N30,000. 

The new N50,000 rate represents an roughly 82% increase, a significant jump that aims to standardize costs but arrives at a time when many households are grappling with inflation, rising living expenses, and subsidy removals.

### Context and Implementation

WAEC recently concluded its 2026 examinations, while NECO is ongoing. The memo explicitly instructs stakeholders to be notified of the change, which will apply to internal SSCE candidates starting with NECO in 2027. 

The adjustment is framed as necessary for the examination bodies to sustain operations, cover increasing costs, and maintain quality standards in a challenging economic environment.

State governments, which often sponsor candidates, particularly in public schools, are expected to bear much of the additional burden. 

Concerns are already mounting that this could exacerbate outstanding debts owed to WAEC and NECO and further strain education budgets across the federation.

The announcement has triggered immediate public discourse. Education advocates and parents worry it may discourage enrollment or push more students out of the formal system, especially in rural areas and low-income families where secondary education is already a significant investment. 

Critics describe it as another blow to access to education in a country striving to meet Sustainable Development Goals on learning outcomes.

Supporters of the hike argue that examination bodies require adequate funding to combat malpractice, improve infrastructure, and deliver credible certificates that hold value internationally. 

Without sustainable financing, the integrity of Nigeria’s most critical exit examinations could suffer.

This development occurs against a backdrop of broader education sector challenges: teacher shortages, inadequate infrastructure, and the lingering effects of past disruptions like the ASUU strikes and COVID-19. 

For a nation with a youthful population where education is often seen as the primary ladder out of poverty, fee increases of this magnitude invite questions about equity and long-term national development.

As families and school administrators begin planning for 2027, the N50,000 threshold looms large. 

Whether state governments will step up with subsidies, scholarships, or other interventions remains to be seen. 

In the meantime, the policy underscores a tough balancing act — between funding quality examinations and ensuring no child is left behind in the pursuit of a secondary school certificate.

The coming months will likely see intensified debate, stakeholder consultations, and possible calls for a review. 

For now, the message from the Ministry is clear: starting next year, registering for WAEC or NECO will come at a steeper cost — one that many Nigerians will feel directly. 

Education remains the bedrock of progress. How this fee adjustment ultimately affects that foundation will define much of the conversation in Nigerian homes and policy circles in the years ahead.
