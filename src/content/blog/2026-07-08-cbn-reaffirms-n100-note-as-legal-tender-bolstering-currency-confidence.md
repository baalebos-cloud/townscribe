**Abuja, Nigeria – July 8, 2026**– In a timely and empowering announcement that underscores Nigeria’s commitment to monetary stability, the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) has firmly reminded the public, businesses, and financial institutions that the standard ₦100 banknote remains fully legal tender alongside its commemorative counterpart. 

This clarification comes amid isolated reports of rejection, providing much-needed reassurance in an economy where every naira counts.

CBN Acting Director of Corporate Communications, Hakama Sidi-Ali, issued the statement emphasizing that both versions of the ₦100 note— the standard design and the 2014 Centenary commemorative edition featuring cultural motifs and security features—must be accepted for all transactions across the country. Violators risk enforcement actions under the CBN Act, as rejecting legal tender undermines public confidence in the national currency.

This directive is more than regulatory housekeeping—it’s a proactive win for everyday Nigerians. 

In a nation where small-denomination notes facilitate everything from market purchases and transportation fares to petty trading and tipping, affirming the ₦100’s status helps maintain smooth cash flow, especially in informal sectors that employ the majority of the workforce.

Veteran observers note that occasional confusion arises from the coexistence of note designs, similar to past transitions with higher denominations. 

By addressing it head-on, the CBN demonstrates responsiveness and a dedication to preserving the naira’s usability amid broader economic reforms. 

This builds trust, discourages arbitrage or hoarding, and supports financial inclusion goals.

Nigeria’s currency history includes successful demonetization efforts and extensions of legal tender status to ease public hardship, as seen in previous old-note policies. 

Here, the issue appears limited rather than widespread—many citizens and traders continue accepting the notes without issue, while some attribute selectivity to wear-and-tear, design familiarity (e.g., centenary vs. standard), or macroeconomic pressures reducing the purchasing power of smaller units.
