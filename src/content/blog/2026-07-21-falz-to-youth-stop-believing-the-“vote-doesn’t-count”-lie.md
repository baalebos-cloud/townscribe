**Lagos, Nigeria – July 21, 2026** – In a powerful and timely message that is resonating across the country, popular Nigerian entertainer, lawyer, and activist Folarin ‘Falz’ Falana has delivered a strong rebuttal to the growing cynicism that one’s vote makes no difference in Nigerian elections.

Speaking passionately, Falz declared: “*Politicians want people to think their votes don’t matter, but they do.”* 

He pointed out a simple but compelling truth — if votes were irrelevant, politicians would not invest enormous resources in influencing outcomes through vote buying, intimidation, manipulation, and other desperate tactics.

Falz’s argument cuts through the widespread disillusionment many young Nigerians feel after years of unfulfilled promises and perceived electoral irregularities. 

*“If it didn’t matter, they would not be doing everything within their power to try and influence results,”* he stressed. 

“*If votes didn’t matter, there would not be vote buying.”* 

He further reasoned that when a large number of citizens move decisively in one direction, it becomes far more difficult for any rigging or manipulation to succeed. 

The message is clear: collective participation can overwhelm attempts to subvert the will of the people.

This intervention comes at a critical moment. With 2027 general elections on the horizon, concerns about voter turnout, electoral reforms, and public trust in the democratic process remain high. 

Many young people, in particular, have expressed fatigue, believing their participation changes little in a system often dominated by money and incumbency power.

As both a celebrated artist (Falz the Badt Guy) and a vocal advocate for good governance, Falz brings a unique credibility to this conversation. 

His blend of entertainment and activism has earned him a massive following among younger Nigerians, making his call to action particularly potent. 

He embodies the idea that citizens — especially the youth — can engage politically without losing their authenticity or voice.

His comments echo broader calls by civil society groups, election observers, and democracy advocates who continue to push for stronger institutions, better voter education, and stricter enforcement against electoral malpractices.

Nigeria’s democracy has made significant strides since 1999, but persistent challenges — including low voter turnout in some cycles, allegations of result manipulation, and the high cost of participation — have fueled apathy. 

Falz flips the script: the very desperation of politicians to control outcomes is proof positive that every vote carries weight.

By encouraging citizens to reject the narrative of helplessness, he is reminding Nigerians of their agency. 

An *“overwhelming majority”* voting conscientiously, he suggests, can shift the balance of power and make subversion far harder.

Having covered Nigerian elections and youth political engagement for many years, I see Falz’s message as both timely and necessary. 

Voter apathy is a silent killer of democracy — it hands power to the most organized and often least scrupulous actors. 

Yet history shows that when citizens awaken and participate en masse, real change becomes possible.

This is not naive optimism. It is strategic realism. Politicians’ heavy investment in rigging, buying, and influencing votes is their own unwitting admission that the ballot remains a potent weapon in the hands of the people.

As we approach future electoral cycles, messages like this — delivered in accessible, culturally resonant ways — could help rekindle civic energy. The power ultimately lies with the people, but only if they choose to exercise it.
