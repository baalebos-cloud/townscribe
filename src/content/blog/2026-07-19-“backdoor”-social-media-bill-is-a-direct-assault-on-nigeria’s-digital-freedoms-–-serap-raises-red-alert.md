**July 19, 2026**- In a bold move that has ignited fresh debate over online rights in Africa’s largest democracy, the Socio-Economic Rights and Accountability Project (SERAP) has issued a strong warning against the Nigeria Data Protection (Amendment) Bill, 2026, currently before the National Assembly. 

The civil society organisation describes the proposed legislation as a ***“backdoor attempt”*** to regulate social media and exert greater government control over digital expression.

In a detailed letter dated July 18, 2026, signed by SERAP Deputy Director Kolawole Oluwadare and addressed to Senate President Godswill Akpabio and House Speaker Tajudeen Abbas, the group demanded the immediate withdrawal of the bill. 

SERAP vowed to pursue legal action if it advances in its current form, citing risks to millions of Nigerians who depend on social media for information, business, education, and civic participation.

Sponsored by Senator Ned Nwoko (APC, Delta North), the amendment seeks to mandate social media platforms, data controllers, and processors operating in Nigeria to establish physical offices within the country. 

Noncompliant entities could face shutdown or prohibition by the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC) after just 30 days. 

Proponents frame it as a measure to enhance data protection and local accountability.

SERAP, however, argues that such localization requirements would hand regulators sweeping powers, exposing local staff to potential retaliation, facilitating political pressure, and making censorship easier. 

The organization warns it could indirectly achieve the same outcome as the 2021 Twitter ban—later ruled unlawful by the ECOWAS Court of Justice—by empowering authorities to block platforms without transparent due process.

The rights group contends the bill lacks essential safeguards: no requirement for prior judicial approval, insufficient opportunities for compliance, and no exploration of less restrictive alternatives. 

It fails constitutional tests of necessity and proportionality under Nigeria’s 1999 Constitution, as well as international obligations under the African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

“*Although the present Bill differs from the Twitter suspension in form, it creates the possibility of achieving the same result indirectly,”* SERAP stated. 

Critics fear it revives elements of previously rejected social media regulation efforts, potentially stifling independent voices, journalists, bloggers, and ordinary citizens in an era where digital platforms are vital lifelines.

This development comes amid ongoing tensions between the government and digital platforms. Past actions, including temporary bans and fines on major tech companies, have drawn accusations of overreach. 

Nigeria boasts one of Africa’s most vibrant digital ecosystems, with millions of young people using social media for everything from entrepreneurship to activism and real-time news.

SERAP’s intervention highlights a recurring pattern: efforts to balance national security, data sovereignty, and public order against fundamental rights to freedom of expression and access to information. 

Similar concerns have surfaced in other regulatory attempts, including cybercrime amendments and content moderation pushes.

As the bill progresses, stakeholders from civil society, tech firms, media organisations, and the public are watching closely. 

SERAP’s letter underscores the need for any regulation to be transparent, proportionate, and developed through broad consultation rather than rushed amendments that could have far-reaching unintended consequences.

For Nigeria — a youthful, digitally native nation — preserving an open internet while addressing legitimate concerns like misinformation, data privacy, and harmful content remains a delicate but critical balance. 

The coming weeks in the National Assembly will test lawmakers’ commitment to democratic values in the digital age.
