**NEW YORK**— In a decisive blow to one of the most powerful and destructive criminal empires in modern history, Ismael "El Mayo" Zambada Garcia, the elusive co-founder of Mexico’s Sinaloa Cartel, was sentenced Monday to life in prison without the possibility of parole in federal court in Brooklyn.

U.S. District Judge Brian Cogan handed down the mandatory sentence following Zambada’s guilty plea last August to leading a continuing criminal enterprise and racketeering charges. 

The 76-year-old admitted responsibility for trafficking at least 1.5 million kilograms of cocaine, along with vast quantities of heroin, fentanyl, and methamphetamine into the United States over nearly four decades.

Prosecutors described Zambada as one of the most prolific narcotraffickers the world has ever seen. 

Alongside his longtime partner Joaquín "**El Chapo**" Guzmán, he built the Sinaloa Cartel into a sophisticated, hyper-violent organization that corrupted Mexican officials at every level through millions in bribes, deployed armed sicarios for torture and murder, and generated billions in illicit profits while poisoning American communities.

*"This chapter closes for good,"* said U.S. Attorney for the Eastern District of New York Joseph Nocella Jr. *"Ismael Zambada Garcia spent nearly four decades poisoning American communities to make billions of dollars in profit and ordering the murders of anyone who stood in his way."*

 **A Life of Crime Ends in U.S. Custody**

Zambada’s downfall came under dramatic circumstances. In July 2024, he was reportedly lured onto a plane in Mexico by one of El Chapo’s sons, Joaquín Guzmán López, and flown to the U.S., where he was taken into custody. 

He pleaded guilty in August 2025, sparing the government a lengthy trial and avoiding the death penalty. As part of the deal, he agreed to a $15 billion forfeiture judgment — one of the largest in U.S. history.

In a statement read in court, Zambada expressed remorse: "*I recognize the grave harm that illegal drugs have done to the people of the United States, of Mexico and elsewhere... I apologize to everyone who has suffered or been affected by my actions."*

His defense highlighted his age, health issues (including age-related cognitive decline), and full acceptance of responsibility, requesting placement in a secure federal medical facility rather than a maximum-security prison like the one housing El Chapo. 

Prosecutors countered that his ongoing influence — including loyalty from cartel members and at least one son still active — poses serious security risks. 

The judge ordered the Bureau of Prisons to consider his medical needs.

This sentencing marks a historic milestone, placing both co-founders of the Sinaloa Cartel behind bars for life in the United States. 

It underscores years of law enforcement efforts to dismantle the organization, which has been linked to countless deaths from drug overdoses and cartel violence on both sides of the border.

Officials emphasized that while one chapter ends, the fight against fentanyl trafficking and cartel power continues. Attorney General Pam Bondi and others hailed it as a key victory in protecting American lives from the scourge of deadly drugs.

For families devastated by the opioid crisis and communities torn by cartel violence, Monday’s outcome brings a measure of justice — though the human toll remains incalculable.
