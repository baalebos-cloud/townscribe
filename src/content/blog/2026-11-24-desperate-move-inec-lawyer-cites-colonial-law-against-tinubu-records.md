**Abuja, Nigeria—November 24, 2026—African** Action Congress presidential candidate Omoyele Sowore on Tuesday accused counsel representing the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC) Chairman of resorting to a colonial-era legal relic in a desperate bid to prevent the disclosure of President Bola Tinubu’s election records.

In a post on X accompanied by video from the Federal High Court in Abuja, Sowore said Senior Advocate of Nigeria Alex Izinyon, who represents INEC Chairman Professor Joash Amupitan, *“had to dredge up an old sedition case just to argue against the production of Tinubu’s election records in court.”*

Sowore described sedition as *“a relic of colonial repression, whose constitutionality has long been rejected by Nigerian courts.” He asked why such extraordinary lengths were being taken to block records that “ought to be available for public scrutiny.”*

The exchange forms part of the ongoing cybercrime and cyberbullying trial in which the Department of State Services is prosecuting Sowore over social media posts made in August 2025. 

In those posts, the activist described President Tinubu as a “criminal” and accused him of misrepresenting the scale of corruption in Nigeria. The case is before Justice Mohammed Garba Umar.

As part of his defence, Sowore’s legal team obtained a subpoena duces tecum directing the INEC Chairman to appear as a witness and produce specific documents. 

These include Tinubu’s INEC Form CF001 (and an accompanying affidavit concerning lost certificates) submitted for the 1999 Lagos State governorship election, as well as the corresponding form for the 2023 presidential election.

INEC, through Izinyon, has applied to set aside the subpoena. The commission argues that the documents as described do not exist in its custody, that the Chairman was not personally served, and that the request is irrelevant to the criminal proceedings and made in bad faith.

Proceedings on Monday had already been stalled by late filing of defence processes. The matter continued on Tuesday amid heightened public interest, with Sowore framing the invocation of the outdated sedition precedent as evidence of resistance to transparency around the President’s electoral paperwork.

The development adds another layer of intensity to a trial that has repeatedly drawn attention for its intersection of free speech, electoral accountability, and institutional pushback. 

Sowore, who is also the 2027 AAC presidential candidate, has consistently maintained that public records of this nature should not be shielded from scrutiny in a democracy.

Observers note that Nigerian courts have in the past questioned the continued relevance of colonial sedition provisions in light of constitutional guarantees of free expression. 

Whether the court will accept the argument or order production of the documents remains to be seen as arguments continue.
