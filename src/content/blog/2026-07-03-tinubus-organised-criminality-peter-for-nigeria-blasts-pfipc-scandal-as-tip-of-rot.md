**Abuja, Nigeria – July 3, 2026** — In a blistering critique that has ignited fresh debate over transparency in President Bola Tinubu’s administration, prominent good governance advocate Peter Akah has labeled the government as the “*definition of organised criminality*.” 

Akah’s remarks, delivered in a widely circulated video commentary, center on the explosive controversy surrounding the Presidential Foreign Intervention Promotion Council (PFIPC), which the Presidency insists does not exist — despite its apparent allocation of over ₦1.3 billion in the 2026 federal budget.

The PFIPC saga erupted publicly in mid-June when Chief of Staff to the President Femi Gbajabiamila issued a disclaimer denouncing Prince Adeniyi Adeyemi Matthew as an impostor falsely claiming to head the non-existent council (also referred to in some contexts as the Presidential Economic Advisory Council or PEAC). 

Gbajabiamila warned diplomatic missions, financial institutions, and the public against engaging with Adeyemi or the purported agency.

Yet the narrative has grown far more complicated. Independent reports confirm that the 2026 Appropriation Act includes a substantial line item for the PFIPC/PEAC under the Presidency totaling approximately ₦1,302,978,784. 

Critics, including civil society group BudgIT, have demanded urgent investigations into how such an allocation slipped through the budgeting process for an agency the government now disavows.

Adeyemi, who reportedly operated from an office in the Federal Secretariat Complex in Abuja, hosted high-profile events including dinners with diplomats and traditional rulers, and maintained an official-looking website and social media presence. 

He has pushed back vigorously, alleging that Gbajabiamila was not only aware of the council but demanded significant sums — including ₦400 million allegedly received via a proxy, an additional ₦200 million, and nearly 48% of a proposed ₦27 billion take-off grant — before relations soured. Adeyemi maintains his appointment was legitimate and has called for an independent probe.

The Presidency has strongly rejected these claims, describing Adeyemi as a “con artist” facing multiple criminal charges, including forgery and impersonation. 

Court documents list Gbajabiamila and others as potential witnesses, with Adeyemi’s arraignment scheduled for late July. 

Authorities say internal collaborators within government institutions enabled the operation for months, and investigations by the DSS, Police, and EFCC are underway to uncover the full extent of any enabling network.

Peter Akah, a vocal human rights activist and critic known for his calls for electoral reform and accountability (often under the moniker Randy Peter or Peter for Nigeria), argues that the contradictions — budgetary provisions, operational activities, and official denials — expose systemic issues far beyond this single scandal.

*“This unfortunately tells you only little about the Tinubu regime,*” Akah stated. “*The Tinubu government is the definition of organised criminality.”* He urged Nigerians to use the 2027 elections to reject the administration at the ballot box.

This controversy lands amid ongoing public frustration with economic hardships, governance challenges, and perceptions of elite impunity in Nigeria. 

Akah’s intervention amplifies voices from opposition figures, civil society, and ordinary citizens questioning how ghost entities can secure massive budget allocations while the Presidency disclaims knowledge.

Supporters of the administration highlight Adeyemi’s legal troubles and portray the allegations as desperate attempts to smear high officials. 

They point to the need for due process and caution against trial by media. Critics, however, see it as symptomatic of deeper rot: weak internal controls, potential budget padding, and questions about who truly benefits from public funds.

The image circulating alongside Akah’s comments — juxtaposing the fiery activist with Gbajabiamila — underscores the personal and political stakes. 

As investigations proceed and the 2027 political season looms, the PFIPC affair risks becoming a defining test of the Tinubu administration’s commitment to transparency and anti-corruption rhetoric.

For now, Nigerians are left grappling with familiar questions: How deep does the rot go, and who will ultimately be held accountable? 

As Akah and others contend, the PFIPC scandal may indeed be exposing only a fraction of larger systemic issues demanding urgent national attention.
