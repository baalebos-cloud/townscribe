In a decisive move signaling his administration’s push toward a modern digital economy, President Bola Ahmed Tinubu has signed the National Identity Management Commission (NIMC) Act 2026 into law. 

The legislation replaces a nearly 20-year-old framework with a secure, digital-first identity system designed to support Nigeria’s ambition of becoming a one-trillion-dollar economy.

Announced late Friday via a detailed thread on his official X account, President Tinubu described the Act as ending an outdated era and ushering in a new one built on trust, inclusion, and security. 

“*That era is over,” he declared, positioning the law as foundational for a “Nigeria we are building.”*

The new law designates NIMC as the Root Certification Authority for Nigeria’s National Public Key Infrastructure. This means stronger digital signatures, secure transactions, and verified identities across the economy. 

Your National Identification Number (NIN) will become mandatory for passports, voter registration, bank accounts, land transactions, telecom services, pensions, insurance, taxes, consumer credit, and virtually all government services. 

A new **General Multipurpose Card** promises “*one card, multiple possibilities*” for seamless verification.

Data protection features prominently: the Act aligns with the Nigerian Data Protection Act, requiring consent for access and restricting use to stated purposes. 

Penalties for abuse are steep — fines up to ₦20 million for companies and minimum five-year jail terms for offences like multiple registrations or impersonation. 

Enforcement powers include court-authorized investigations, searches, seizures, and decryption.

Special provisions target vulnerable groups, including those without fixed addresses, while diaspora Nigerians gain easier access to identity services. 

The NIMC Governing Board gains broader representation from key agencies and ensures geopolitical balance.

 A unified, secure digital identity could slash leakages, boost financial inclusion, ease diaspora engagement, and support everything from elections to welfare delivery.

Yet history cautions against euphoria. Success hinges on implementation: data security in a country with past breaches, accessibility for the poorest and least connected, and resistance to mission creep or elite capture. Heavy penalties are welcome, but enforcement must be even-handed.

President Tinubu framed the law as “*Renewed Hope Agenda at work,”* crediting the National Assembly, NIMC, and partners. 

For ordinary Nigerians weary of reforms that feel extractive rather than empowering, this will be judged not by signatures or tweets, but by tangible outcomes — fewer scams, smoother services, and real inclusion.

In a nation racing toward digital transformation while many still battle basic survival, the NIMC Act 2026 is both ambitious and contentious. 

It could mark a turning point toward efficiency and trust — or become another well-intentioned policy lost in execution gaps. Nigerians will be watching closely.
