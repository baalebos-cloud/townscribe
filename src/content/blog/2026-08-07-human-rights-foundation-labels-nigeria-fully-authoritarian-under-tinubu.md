**Abuja, Nigeria—August 7, 2026—The** Human Rights Foundation (HRF) has classified Nigeria as a fully authoritarian regime in its latest Tyranny Tracker assessment, arguing that the country’s electoral process, protection of dissent, and institutional checks on executive power have deteriorated to levels inconsistent with democratic governance.

The classification places Nigeria in the most restrictive of HRF’s three categories—democratic, hybrid authoritarian, or fully authoritarian—alongside countries the organization regards as lacking meaningful pathways for peaceful transfers of power or effective constraints on those controlling the state.



**What the Assessment Found**

HRF evaluates countries across three core areas: electoral competition, freedom of dissent, and institutional accountability. 

Its framework examines 45 indicators using qualitative analysis rather than relying solely on numerical scores. The Tyranny Tracker was launched in February 2026.

According to the foundation, Nigeria’s democratic institutions have been weakened by the growing concentration of political power, patronage politics, insecurity, and the limited ability of state institutions to hold the executive accountable. 

While acknowledging the country’s longest uninterrupted stretch of civilian rule since the return to democracy in 1999, HRF argued that the continuation of elections has not translated into stronger democratic safeguards.

The organization raised concerns about the 2023 presidential election that brought President Bola Tinubu to power, describing it as flawed due to legal challenges, violence, alleged irregularities, voter disenfranchisement, and manipulation. 

It also alleged that the political environment gives the ruling party significant advantages, including restrictions on opposition campaigns, security agency interference, and unequal access to state resources and media.

HRF further questioned the independence of the Independent National Electoral Commission, citing politically controversial appointments and executive influence over funding.



**Freedom of Expression and Civic Space**

The assessment highlighted what it described as increasing intimidation, arrests, prosecution and censorship of journalists, civil society organisations, opposition figures, activists and ordinary citizens who criticise government policies. 

It referenced the August 2024 nationwide **\#EndBadGovernance** protests, the use of the Cybercrimes Act against online critics, and restrictions on critical broadcasting, including action against a protest song by musician Eedris Abdulkareem.

Beyond expression, HRF expressed concern over the effectiveness of the judiciary and legislature as checks on executive authority. 

It pointed to delays and dismissals of election petitions, questions around compliance with ECOWAS Court of Justice rulings, and the National Assembly’s March 2025 approval of emergency rule in Rivers State via a voice vote. 

The foundation also alleged a broader culture of impunity surrounding security personnel accused of human rights violations.



**Context and Differing Assessments**

HRF’s classification represents the organisation’s independent assessment and is not a formal determination by an intergovernmental body. 

Other major indexes present a different picture. Freedom House’s Freedom in the World 2026 report continues to rate Nigeria as **“Partly Free,”** with a score of 44 out of 100.

The Nigerian government has previously rejected international criticism of its human rights record and governance practices, maintaining that democratic institutions remain intact and that actions against individuals are grounded in law rather than political persecution.

As debates continue over electoral reform, civic space, judicial independence and the balance of power, HRF’s assessment adds to a growing body of international and domestic commentary examining the state of Nigeria’s democracy more than a quarter-century after the end of military rule.
