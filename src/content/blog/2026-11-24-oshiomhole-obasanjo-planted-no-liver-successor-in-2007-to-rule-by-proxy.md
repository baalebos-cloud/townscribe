Senator Adams Oshiomhole has stirred fresh debate over Nigeria’s 2007 power transition, claiming former President Olusegun Obasanjo deliberately selected a successor he believed lacked the courage to resist remote control from Abeokuta. 

Speaking on Tuesday at the Abuja launch of Sam Omatseye’s book **From AD to APC: The Evolution of Nigerian Political Parties and the Exploits of One Man (1998–2015)**, the Edo North senator delivered a pointed recount of political maneuvers that still shape Nigeria’s democracy.

According to Oshiomhole, Obasanjo *“planted someone who he believes has no liver that if he signals him he will run to Abeokuta to take orders.”* 

That “someone,” the senator made clear, was the late President Umaru Musa Yar’Adua. Oshiomhole recalled that the National Assembly had even passed a law allowing the indefinite postponement of elections if a presidential candidate died—a provision that became relevant amid intense speculation about Yar’Adua’s health during the campaign.

The senator invoked the now-famous moment when Obasanjo publicly telephoned Yar’Adua: ***“Umaru, are you dead? Umaru, are you alive?”*** When Yar’Adua survived and the election proceeded, Obasanjo, in Oshiomhole’s telling, believed he had secured a presidency ***“by proxy.”*** 

Yet the plan unravelled. After Yar’Adua’s death in 2010, then-Vice President Goodluck Jonathan assumed office and eventually grew weary of what Oshiomhole described as relentless pressure. 

*“When you overharass someone at a point he will completely avoid you,”* the senator said. *“I think it is at that point he tore his (PDP) card.”*

The remarks come at a politically charged moment. With the 2027 general elections already dominating national conversation, Oshiomhole’s account revisits one of the most contested succession stories of the Fourth Republic. 

Obasanjo has previously defended his choice, stating he obtained medical clearance confirming Yar’Adua’s fitness after a successful kidney transplant and denied any plot to install a puppet.

Oshiomhole’s intervention at the book launch—an event focused on the journey from the Alliance for Democracy through the Action Congress to the All Progressives Congress—places the 2007 episode firmly within the longer arc of party realignments and power struggles that produced today’s political landscape. 

The senator, a former labor leader, two-term Edo governor, and one-time APC national chairman, spoke with the familiar blend of candor and theatrical delivery that has defined his public life.

Whether the claim will prompt a response from the former president or simply fuel another cycle of historical score-settling remains to be seen. 

What is certain is that nearly two decades later, the question of who truly controlled the levers of power after 2007 continues to provoke strong opinions across Nigeria’s political class.
