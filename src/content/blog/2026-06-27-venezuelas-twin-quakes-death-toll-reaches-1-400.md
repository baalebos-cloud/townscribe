**Caracas, Venezuela** — In a devastating blow to a country already strained by years of challenges, two powerful earthquakes struck northern Venezuela on Wednesday evening, June 24, 2026, unleashing destruction that has claimed at least 1,430 lives, injured more than 3,200 others, and left thousands missing or homeless.

The rare "seismic doublet" — a 7.2-magnitude foreshock followed just 39 seconds later by a 7.5-magnitude mainshock near Morón on the Caribbean coast — ranks among the strongest tremors to hit Venezuela in over a century. 

The quakes devastated coastal La Guaira state, hardest hit, while shaking the capital Caracas and surrounding regions, toppling buildings, cracking infrastructure, and triggering widespread panic.

Families are enduring anguished waits outside mounds of rubble, calling out names of loved ones still trapped. 

In La Guaira’s Catia La Mar and Caraballeda, residents describe the violent shaking that lasted what felt like an eternity: "*We kept falling to the floor; things were falling on us*," one survivor recounted. 

Many spent nights camping outdoors, fearing aftershocks — at least 430 have rattled the region since.

Tragic losses include young soccer talents like 18-year-old Yimvert Berroteran, who had recently played in youth World Cups, along with other players. In one heartbreaking case, the partner of a Zulia FC defender died shielding their toddler.

Hospitals, long neglected, are overwhelmed. Decades of underinvestment have left medical facilities struggling to treat the injured surge, compounding frustration among residents desperate for faster aid and answers.

Rescuers are in a desperate race against time and aftershocks, hampered by limited heavy machinery. 

International help is pouring in: U.S. teams, including Miami-Dade’s 80-member Florida Task Force 1 with canine units, French civil security forces, and over 1,600 additional rescuers from multiple nations. Acting President Delcy Rodríguez announced 10 more countries joining efforts.

One runway at Simón Bolívar International Airport near Caracas is now operational, easing aid delivery bottlenecks. 

The U.S. has coordinated closely with interim Venezuelan authorities, deploying mobile hospitals, Starlink terminals, and the USS Fort Lauderdale for medical and logistics support. 

A new emergency hotline (0800-RESCATE) has been activated for missing persons reports and volunteer coordination.

In La Guaira, 60% of electricity has been restored, though mountain infrastructure repairs remain challenging. Some scavenging in damaged buildings has been reported amid the chaos, but National Guard patrols are visible.

Satellite imagery reveals extensive devastation in La Guaira, with collapsed apartment blocks like the Petunia Building in Caracas symbolizing the widespread structural failures. 

Estimates suggest billions in housing and economic damage — potentially 6% of GDP — not counting full infrastructure losses.

This disaster strikes at a vulnerable moment for Venezuela, amplifying existing strains on infrastructure and services. 

Yet, it has also spotlighted resilience: locals digging by hand alongside professionals, volunteers stepping up despite personal losses, and a global outpouring of support.

As aftershocks continue and the death toll may still rise, the coming weeks will test the nation’s recovery capacity. 

Humanitarian organizations urge donations and caution; temporary shelters are being set up, but anxiety lingers as people balance hope for survivors with the grim reality of rebuilding shattered lives and communities.
