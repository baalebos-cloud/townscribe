On Thursday, 11 June 2026, the first batch of 258 Nigerian voluntary returnees, including women and young children, stepped onto Nigerian soil after being evacuated from South Africa amid a surge in xenophobic tensions and anti-migrant unrest.

The Nigeria Immigration Service, working seamlessly with other agencies, handled immigration clearance with calm efficiency. Officers guided the weary travellers through formalities before handing them over to the National Commission for Refugees, Migrants and Internally Displaced Persons (NCFRMI) for further support and documentation. 

The reception was led by the Minister of Humanitarian Affairs and Poverty Alleviation, Dr. Bernard M. Doro, and the Minister of State for Foreign Affairs, Ambassador Sola Enikanolaiye, alongside representatives from NEMA, NIDCOM, and other key stakeholders.

This evacuation is no routine diplomatic exercise. It forms part of an emergency Federal Government initiative to bring home distressed citizens following violent anti-migrant protests in South Africa. 

High unemployment — hovering above 30 percent in parts of the country — has fuelled demands for the deportation of foreign nationals, with African migrants, including Nigerians, bearing the brunt of attacks, harassment, and document checks that sometimes turn exploitative.

Many of the returnees had built lives in South Africa over years. Some were professionals with valid documents that had expired or faced renewal hurdles; others had overstayed amid the uncertainty. 

What united them was a shared decision to leave before the situation deteriorated further. 

The Nigerian consulate in South Africa reports that over 1,000 citizens have already registered for repatriation, with additional flights approved.

For some returnees, the moment they set foot back home brought visible relief. “No place like home,” one woman told reporters, her voice thick with emotion. 

“At least now I can sleep without fear.” Others carried the heavier burden of what they had left behind — businesses, relationships, and futures suddenly uncertain. 

Children clutched small bags and toys, their faces a mixture of exhaustion and curiosity about a country many had only heard about from parents.

The government has pledged immediate reintegration support, including cash assistance, transport to their states of origin, and livelihood aid. 

Officials have been careful to stress that none of the returnees are criminals — they are ordinary people caught in a wave of continental migration tensions that has also seen other African nations organise similar returns.

The smooth operation at MMIA reflects the Renewed Hope Agenda’s emphasis on protecting Nigerians wherever they are. President Bola Ahmed Tinubu’s administration has framed the exercise as citizen diplomacy in practice — a signal that no Nigerian will be abandoned when safety is threatened abroad. 

Diplomatic channels with South Africa remain open, even as both countries navigate the sensitive politics of migration, economic frustration, and national identity.

Yet the episode also exposes deeper realities. African migrants often move across borders in search of opportunity, only to find themselves scapegoated when local economies falter. 

Nigeria’s own economic challenges mean that reintegrating hundreds of returnees will test systems already under strain. 

The joy of safe arrival is real; the question of sustainable livelihoods back home lingers for many families.

This first successful flight is more than a logistical achievement. It underscores Nigeria’s growing willingness to use state resources to shield its diaspora when bilateral relations or local conditions turn hostile. 

It also highlights the human cost of xenophobia — families uprooted, children displaced, professionals forced to restart from scratch.

For the returnees now being processed and supported, the coming weeks will determine whether “home” becomes a place of genuine renewal or simply a temporary refuge. 

For the wider Nigerian public, the images of compatriots stepping off that plane serve as a reminder of both the vulnerabilities faced by citizens abroad and the capacity of their government to respond when it matters most.

More flights are expected in the days ahead. The process may be orderly and dignified on the tarmac, but behind every face in the crowd lies a personal story of courage, loss, and the enduring search for safety and dignity on African soil.
