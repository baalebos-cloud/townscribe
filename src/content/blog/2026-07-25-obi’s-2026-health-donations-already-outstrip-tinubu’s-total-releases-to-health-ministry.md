**Abuja/Lagos – July 25, 2026** — Nigeria’s leading opposition figure, Peter Obi, has once again put his personal resources behind grassroots healthcare, donating ₦10 million to the Catholic Maternity Hospital Amunaya in Ogoja, Cross River State, to boost maternal and child health services. 

The gesture has triggered sharp political commentary, with social media commentator Sadiq Maunde claiming that Obi’s cumulative healthcare donations in 2026 alone already exceed the total funds released by the Bola Tinubu administration to the Federal Ministry of Health.

Maunde made the comparison shortly after the donation became public. ***“Peter Obi donated ₦10 million today again to a maternity hospital. The money Peter Obi spent this year alone on healthcare is far more than what Tinubu released to the Ministry of Health,”*** he stated. 

The commentator framed the interventions as evidence of a private citizen’s stronger practical commitment to the sector than official government funding has shown so far this year.

Obi, the Nigeria Democratic Congress (NDC) presidential candidate, presented the ₦10 million during a visit to the Catholic Maternity Hospital Amunaya. The funds are earmarked to support maternal and child healthcare delivery at the facility. 

The visit formed part of broader engagements in Cross River, where he also supported education initiatives and encouraged voter registration ahead of the INEC deadline.

This is not an isolated act. Throughout 2026, Obi has repeatedly visited and supported hospitals, nursing institutions, and primary healthcare facilities across several states. Earlier this month he donated another ₦10 million to St. Raphael’s Hospital in Umueri, Anambra State, while marking his 65th birthday, emphasizing that birthdays should be occasions for service rather than self-indulgence. Similar contributions have gone to facilities in Imo, Enugu, and other locations in recent months.

Supporters view the pattern as consistent with Obi’s long-standing focus on human capital development, particularly in health and education. 

Critics of the comparison argue that private philanthropy, however welcome, cannot substitute for systematic government funding and policy reform.

The Tinubu administration has defended its overall economic reforms and budgetary priorities, insisting that efforts are underway to strengthen public services despite fiscal constraints. 

Officials have not issued an immediate detailed response to Maunde’s specific claim about 2026 releases to the Health Ministry.

The remarks have nevertheless ignited fresh debate online and among political stakeholders. 

Many Nigerians point to persistent challenges in public hospitals—shortages of essential drugs, equipment gaps, and inadequate maternal care infrastructure—as reasons why both private contributions and stronger official funding matter. 

Others caution against equating individual donations with the scale and responsibility of federal budget implementation.

Maunde maintained that greater investment in healthcare remains essential if Nigeria is to improve outcomes for ordinary citizens, especially women and children who rely on facilities such as the Ogoja maternity hospital.

Peter Obi has positioned healthcare and education as central pillars of national development in his public advocacy. 

His continued personal donations keep the conversation alive about the adequacy of official funding and the role of private citizens in filling gaps. 

As the political season intensifies toward 2027, such gestures are likely to remain both practical interventions and political statements.

Whether the comparison of private donations to government releases holds under detailed budgetary scrutiny will depend on transparent figures from the Ministry of Health and the Budget Office. 

For now, the latest ₦10 million gift has once again placed the state of Nigeria’s health sector under the spotlight.
